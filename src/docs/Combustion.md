# Combustion of Wildland Fuels


## Heat Contents of Wood (BTU per Pound)

A completely combusted, bone-dry pound of wood produces approximately 8,600 BTUs (or about 9.07 megajoules / 2.52 kilowatt-hours) of total heat energy. While 8,600 BTUs is the standard scientific baseline across nearly all wood species on a dry-weight basis, the exact output varies slightly due to natural chemical differences and water content.

### Hardwoods (e.g., Oak, Maple, Birch)
Hardwoods average roughly 8,600 Btu/lb when oven-dry. They do not contain significant pitch or resins, keeping their energy density very consistent.

### Softwoods (e.g., Pine, Fir, Spruce)
Softwoods typically yield 8,600 to 9,600 Btu/lb when oven-dry. Natural resins, saps, and pitch burn hotter than wood fiber, giving softwoods a slightly higher heat per pound.

## Moisture Effects on Heat of Combustion

For seasoned firewood at 20% moisture content, heat of combustion drops to roughly 6,500-7,000 Btu/lb.Water inside the wood absorbs energy to boil away as steam, reducing the net usable heat output.

### Step-by-Step Energy Breakdown

It takes approximately 1,120 to 1,200 BTUs of heat energy to warm and evaporate 1 pound of water from wood during combustion, with 970 BTUs required just for the phase change from liquid to steam.

Before water leaves the wood as steam, the fire must supply energy across two distinct stages, plus a small amount to break cellular bonds:

- *Heating the Water (~150 BTUs)*: Liquid water inside wood starts near ambient temperature (around 60°F). Because 1 BTU raises 1 pound of water by 1°F, heating it to the boiling point of 212°F requires roughly 152 BTUs.

- *Vaporizing the Water (~970 BTUs)*: Once at 212°F, changing 1 pound of liquid water into steam requires 970 BTUs of latent heat. This energy breaks liquid bonds without raising the temperature further.

- *Overcoming Wood Bonds (~20 to 80 BTUs)*: Water chemically bound inside wood cell walls (hygroscopic water) requires slightly more energy to release than free surface water, pushing the real-world total to roughly 1,150 to 1,200 BTUs per pound.

The optimal temperature for clean, complete wood combustion is between 1,100°F and 1,500°F (593°C to 816°C), and the standard heat of combustion for dry wood is approximately 8,600 BTUs per pound (20 MJ/kg).

## Optimal Combustion Temperature Zones

Wood does not burn all at once; instead, it goes through distinct temperature stages as it heats up:
• Below 500°F (260°C) — Moisture Evaporation & Pyrolysis: Water boils off first, followed by the thermal breakdown of wood fibers into volatile gases, smoke, and charcoal.
• 500°F to 1,100°F (260°C to 593°C) — Incomplete Combustion: Volatile wood gases begin igniting, but temperatures are too low to burn all hydrocarbons. Much of the fuel escapes up the flue as unburned smoke and creosote.
• 1,100°F to 1,500°F (593°C to 816°C) — Optimal Secondary Combustion: Nearly all volatile wood gases and particulates ignite cleanly in the presence of oxygen. This range maximizes heat extraction while producing minimal visible smoke.
• Above 1,600°F (871°C) — Over-Firing Risk: Extreme firebox temperatures can warp steel, crack cast iron, and damage chimney liners without significantly improving combustion efficiency. (Note: Catalytic wood stoves lower the gas-ignition threshold to roughly 500°F, allowing clean burns at lower firebox temperatures.)

## Heat of Combustion (Higher vs. Lower Heating Value)

Engineers measure wood's heat of combustion in two ways depending on whether water vapor is captured:
• Higher Heating Value (HHV): Roughly 8,600 BTUs/lb (20.0 MJ/kg) for bone-dry hardwood and 8,600 to 9,600 BTUs/lb (20.0 to 22.3 MJ/kg) for resinous softwoods like pine. This assumes all heat is recovered, including the latent heat from condensing exhaust water vapor.
• Lower Heating Value (LHV): Roughly 7,500 to 8,000 BTUs/lb (17.5 to 18.6 MJ/kg) for bone-dry wood. Because hydrogen in wood combines with oxygen during burning to create new water vapor—which escapes up the chimney in standard stoves—LHV represents the realistic maximum heat available before accounting for wood moisture or appliance efficiency.

## Key Conditions for Optimal Combustion

To sustain the 1,100°F to 1,500°F target zone, a fire relies on three factors (often called the "Three T's"):
• Temperature: Insulated fireboxes (lined with firebrick or refractory baffles) keep heat concentrated around the gases rather than letting cold metal walls quench the flame.
• Turbulence: Secondary air tubes or baffles mix fresh, preheated oxygen directly into rising wood gases.
• Time: Smoke and gases need enough residence time inside the hot firebox to finish burning before entering the cooler flue pipe.
For thermodynamics and biomass engineering calculations, dry wood is typically modeled using a standard ultimate chemical composition of roughly 50% Carbon, 6% Hydrogen, and 44% Oxygen by mass, which governs both its stoichiometric air requirements and adiabatic flame temperature.

1. Standard Engineering Reference Constants

When setting up energy and mass balances for dry wood (specifically pine/softwood vs. general lignocellulosic biomass), use these standard parameters:
• Ultimate Analysis (Dry, Ash-Free Basis): ~50.0% C, 6.0% H, 43.5% O, 0.2% N, <0.1% S, with roughly 0.3% to 1.0% ash (resin-rich pine trends slightly higher in carbon and hydrogen).
• Higher Heating Value (\(\text{HHV}_{\text{dry}}\)): 20.0 to 22.3 MJ/kg (8,600 to 9,600 BTU/lb) for pine.
• Lower Heating Value (\(\text{LHV}_{\text{dry}}\)): Calculated by subtracting the latent heat of water formed from hydrogen combustion (\(\approx 9 \times \text{H}_{\text{mass fraction}} \times h_{fg}\)):
\(\text{LHV}_{\text{dry}}=\text{HHV}_{\text{dry}}-2.442\times (9\cdot Y_{\text{H}})\)
(For 6% hydrogen, \(9 \times 0.06 = 0.54\text{ kg H}_2\text{O/kg dry fuel}\), subtracting roughly 1.32 MJ/kg or 567 BTU/lb).

2. Moisture Adjustments (As-Received Basis)

In engineering literature, moisture is tracked on either a wet basis (\(MC_{w}\)) or dry basis (\(MC_{d}\)). Be careful which convention your moisture meter or dataset uses:
• Conversion: \(MC_w = \frac{MC_d}{1 + MC_d}\)
• Net As-Received Lower Heating Value (\(\text{LHV}_{\text{ar}}\)):
\(\text{LHV}_{\text{ar}}=\text{LHV}_{\text{dry}}(1-MC_{w})-h_{fg}\cdot MC_{w}\)
(Using \(h_{fg} \approx 2.442\text{ MJ/kg}\) at \(25^{\circ }\text{C}\) reference temperature).

3. Stoichiometry & Adiabatic Flame Temperature

• Stoichiometric Air-to-Fuel Ratio (\(A/F_{\text{stoich}}\)): Approximately 6.0 to 6.5 kg of dry air per kg of bone-dry wood (requiring roughly 1.38 to 1.50 kg of \(\text{O}_{2}\) per kg of wood).
• Excess Air (\(\lambda \)): Practical wood combustion requires 30% to 100% excess air (\(\lambda = 1.3 \text{ to } 2.0\)) to achieve complete mixing and prevent CO formation.
• Adiabatic Flame Temperature (\(T_{\text{ad}}\)):
	• Bone-dry wood at stoichiometric air (\(\lambda = 1.0\)): ~1,950°C to 2,050°C (3,540°F to 3,720°F).
	• Seasoned wood (20% \(MC_{w}\)) with 50% excess air (\(\lambda = 1.5\)): Drops to ~1,250°C to 1,400°C (2,280°F to 2,550°F).
	• Green wood (50% \(MC_{w}\)) with 100% excess air (\(\lambda = 2.0\)): Drops below ~850°C to 950°C (1,560°F to 1,740°F), near the quenching limit for sustained flame propagation.
